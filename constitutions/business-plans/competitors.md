# Competitors

Who else the customer could choose, what each one charges, and where the plan's product wins
and loses against them.

## Who counts

Three kinds of alternative, and the plan names all of them:

- **Direct** — a similar product sold to the same customer for the same problem.
- **Indirect** — a different product the customer uses for the same problem: a general tool
  bent to fit, a consultancy, an adjacent platform.
- **The status quo** — spreadsheets, manual work, a person hired to do it, or living with
  the problem. It is always present, and it is usually what the first customers are won
  from.

*We have no competitors* is not a finding. An investor hears either that there is no market
or that nobody looked.

A large platform that could ship the same thing as a feature is listed as well, with what
would stop it — it is the question every investor asks about a young company, and the plan
answers it before it is asked.

The research file may hold every alternative found. The comparison the deck shows picks the
three to seven that customers actually weigh, and says how they were picked.

## Each competitor

A row per competitor carries: name and link; what it sells and to whom; size signals —
funding raised, headcount, customer count — each with source and date; and its price.

## Prices

A published price is recorded as published: each tier, the price metric (per seat, per
unit of usage, per outcome, flat), the billing period and any annual discount, the currency,
the page it came from and the date it was read.

A price that is not published is recorded as *contact sales*. An estimate from a secondary
source — a review site, a procurement database, a customer interview — is marked as an
estimate with that source. A figure nobody published and nobody sourced does not appear.

Published prices are not comparable with one another: a per-seat plan, a usage plan and an
enterprise quote measure different things. Every alternative is therefore also priced as
**the annual cost to the reference customer** defined in [market](market.md), with the
arithmetic shown, and that is the column compared. The status quo is priced the same way —
the hours it takes times what those hours cost — and it is often the anchor the plan's own
price is set against.

The plan's own price sits in the same table, computed the same way, from the
`business-model.md` it is set in.

| Alternative | Price as published (read 2026-09-01) | Annual cost, reference customer (12 seats) |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor A | $39/seat/month, annual billing ([pricing](https://example.com/pricing)) | 12 × 39 × 12 = $5,616 |
| Competitor B | Contact sales; ~$15k/yr (estimate, three customer interviews) | ~$15,000 |
| Status quo | 20 staff-hours a month at a $45 loaded rate | 20 × 45 × 12 = $10,800 |
| This product | $500/month flat up to 15 seats | $6,000 |

## The comparison

Its columns are the criteria the customer buys on — taken from customer interviews and
lost deals — not the features the product happens to win. A criterion on which a competitor
beats the product stays in, and says so; a table the product wins on every row reads as one
built to be won.

Cells carry facts where facts exist: a limit, an integration, a certification, a time to
set up. A tick against a cross hides the difference an investor is asking about.

A two-by-two with the product alone in the top-right corner is left out unless both axes
are buying criteria and every position is sourced.

## Why the lead holds

A feature lead lasts until a competitor ships the feature. The section says what keeps
the advantage once competitors respond: proprietary data, network effects, switching
costs, a distribution channel others lack, or a cost structure they cannot match. A plan
with none of these says so, and says what it builds toward.
