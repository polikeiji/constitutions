# Market

Who the plan sells to, how many of them there are, and what they are worth a year — the
segment, and its size as TAM, SAM and SOM.

## The segment

A segment is defined by attributes a public source counts: industry, company size or
household type, geography, and the role that feels the problem. *SMBs* and *developers* are
not segments; *US and Canadian accounting firms with 5–50 staff* is, because a census table
says how many there are.

The segment names the user and the economic buyer separately when they differ. The person
who suffers the problem and the person who signs the purchase order are sold to differently,
and a plan that conflates them has a go-to-market that reaches the wrong one.

The **beachhead** is the first segment — narrow enough that the company can become the
obvious choice in it. Later segments are listed as expansion with sizes of their own, and are
not added into the beachhead's figures.

## The reference customer

One concrete, typical customer in the beachhead — its size, how many seats or how much usage
it would buy — is described once here. The competitor price comparison, the business
model's price and the unit economics all use it, so a price per seat and a price per API
call can be compared at all.

## Size

Each tier is a count of customers multiplied by what one pays a year:

- **TAM** — every customer who has the problem, anywhere, at the plan's price.
- **SAM** — the part the product and channel as planned can reach: the geographies,
  languages, segments and regulatory regimes the plan actually serves.
- **SOM** — the customers the plan wins by Y5. It is the Y5 customer count from
  [financials](financials.md), not a percentage picked out of the SAM; a SOM that disagrees
  with the revenue model is two plans.

The bottom-up build leads: a sourced count of customers times the plan's own annual price.
A top-down figure — an analyst's market total narrowed down to the segment — is a
cross-check, and where the two disagree materially, the section says why.

> SAM: 48,000 accounting firms with 5–50 staff in the US and Canada (each country's business
> census, linked, with its year) × $6,000 a year = $288M.

not

> The global AI market is worth $500B. Capturing 1% of it gives us $5B.

The second figure measures a category, not a set of buyers, and 1% of it is a number
nobody can plan a sales team against.

## Sources

An analyst figure names the report, the publisher, the year and what its market definition
includes. A market labelled *AI software* that counts chips and consulting is not the TAM of
a product that sells neither. A growth rate is sourced the same way and says what drives it.

A market that is new — one the product creates — has no report to cite. Its size is built
from the spend it replaces: the hours, headcount or tools the customer pays for today to
cope with the problem, which is the same status-quo cost [competitors](competitors.md)
prices.
